# Introduction

## 1.1 Problem Statement and Motivation

Software engineering has transformed extensively in the past few years as a result of cloud native technologies and DevOps practices, and organizations now deal with significant time pressures to deliver applications quicker while maintaining quality and operational efficiency. This pressure has resulted in a significant interest in automating aspects of the Software Development Lifecycle, where traditional manual processes have been displaced by more complex automated pipelines.
Two approaches that have been implemented and are being advanced simultaneously are serverless computing architectures, such as Azure Functions, and container orchestration like Kubernetes. These two approaches offer different benefits, but the trade-offs make it difficult for development teams and organizations to feel confident in their choices when creating their cloud strategy.
The serverless model has the appeal of complete abstraction of infrastructure, where the developer can focus on the business logic instead of managing servers, scaling decisions, or infrastructure. Microsoft has implemented serverless Function as a Service (FaaS) through Azure Functions, where event-driven execution models are delivered with automatic scaling and pay-per-execution pricing.
Kubernetes has risen to prominence as the de facto container orchestration solution, giving organizations and developers complete control over how to deploy, scale, and manage applications, with portability options between cloud providers. Working knowledge of cloud native applications has matured to the point where companies have eliminated the need to run the Kubernetes control plane by leveraging managed options like Azure Kubernetes Service (AKS).
Even with the enormous amount of documentation and case studies available on the capabilities of both platforms, there is not enough practical comparative research that highlights side-by-side implementations from real-world case-based scenarios. Most of the current literature emphasizes either theoretical benefits or stand-alone performance metrics and fails to analyze all the factors taken into consideration when implementing architectural decisions.
This study aims to generate evidence for both approaches by analyzing the complete software development lifecycle implementation. This includes not only the technical performance characteristics, but also operational aspects such as development effort, operational burden, and maintenance considerations.
The motivation for this study was based on observations within the software development community when teams need to select a platform; many of the decisions were driven by factors deemed relevant to software engineers. Serverless computing is an emerging platform type which gains traction in the software development community based on its simplicity of use and service model; however, there are still questions around whether serverless computing is a great solution for certain application types and traffic patterns. Additionally, while Kubernetes provides powerful capabilities, development teams express concerns related to its complexity and operational burden.
## 1.2 Research Aims and Questions

The objective of this research is to provide an analysis between serverless and container-based approaches to automate Software Development Lifecycle within the Microsoft Azure technology ecosystem to demonstrate practical research that can be used to assist software engineers with architecture decisions in real-life situations. The main research question that drives this study is: "With regards to modern web applications, how do serverless architectures using Azure Functions compare to container-based solutions using Azure Kubernetes Service, when looked at through the lenses of implementation complexity, performance characteristics, operational considerations and cost efficiency?"
The main question branches into multiple secondary research questions that examine specific aspects of the comparison.
How does the implementation complexity differ when using Azure Functions versus Azure Kubernetes Service when building a complete web application (with authentication, data persistence and API endpoints)? This question considers the developer experience, the setup requirements and time to market.
What are the performance characteristics experienced with each approach under changing loads, and how do cold starts, scaling behaviors and resource consumption factor into the experience? For this research, performance focuses on the quantifiable aspects of performance that have impact on the user experience.
What are the operational requirements of each approach (considering monitoring, troubleshooting, maintenance and security management)? This consideration reflects the operational burden of each platform over the longer term, looking at ongoing lessons learned.
For the two approaches, what are the cost implications through varying usage patterns, and how does each pricing model contribute to determining the total cost of ownership of applications with differing traffic loads? This work can provide useful information when considering budgeting and planning to maximize cost effectiveness. What are the major differences in the Continuous Integration and Continuous Deployment pipeline implementations for each approach, and how does this impact their efficiency when considering development workflows and broader automation focus over the software development life cycle? Asking these questions in a combined manner allows for considering each option as a whole and drawing conclusions based on the organizational selection process.
## 1.3 Scope and Limitations

This research focus is restricted to the Microsoft Azure cloud system, using Azure Functions as the serverless implementation, and Azure Kubernetes Service as the container-based option. Although this platform focus may impact the applicability of findings with respect to other cloud offerings, it allows for deeper comparative analysis within a single platform and ecosystem, while providing relevant guidance for organizations who are already using, or considering the Azure platform.
The study implements a representative web app which has components that are common within modern business web applications, including a React-based frontend using TypeScript; a Node.js backend with Express framework, PostgreSQL-based data storage, and authentication mechanisms to protect data. For the serverless implementation, Azure AD B2C will be used for identity management, while the Kubernetes approach will utilize Keycloak to implement a self-hosted authentication option.
Geographically, this study is limited to the Azure regions that are available from a research perspective, and has focused on European data centers. The analysis will not explore network latency characteristics or compliance issues in other regions.
In terms of timeframe, the research captures implementation and testing activities that took place over a period of months including performance data monitoring and broader operational experiences throughout that time. It does not explore long-term trends, seasonal cycles, or longer duration operational patterns.
There are several technical limitations to this study. Although the performance testing uses synthetic workloads that were specifically designed to simulate typical use patterns, these may not necessarily include every conceivable scenario of the applications' behavior and all user interaction models. The testing was conducted under academic Azure subscriptions and may be limited in terms of resources and testing time that the subscription offers.
The comparisons reflect technical and operational considerations, but also recognize that organizational factors would apply including existing levels of team knowledge and expertise in the technology stack, company policies for using specific tools in particular circumstances, and strategic decisions around technology directions. This will be outlined in the discussion section, but not included in substantial quantitative analysis.
The security analysis is limited to standard implementation practices that would be aligned to common definitions of Standard Operating Environment but does not include full penetration testing or enhanced threat modeling workshops. Compliance analysis with regard to any particular industry or standards is beyond the scope of this analysis.
The cost analysis is based on standard Azure pricing models published during the research period and does not apply to the organization purchasing an enterprise license or promotional offer.
## 1.4 Overview of Research Methodology

This research utilizes a comparative case study methodology to create functionally equivalent systems using both serverless and container-based architectures. The methodology combines quantitative performance analysis with qualitative analysis of implementation experience and operational characteristics.
The experimental design employs a controlled comparison approach, where the functionality is implemented with the same API endpoints and user interface. This method ensures that differences in performance can be attributed to differences in the platforms and not functional differences.
The implementation work is completed incrementally, with each platform being independently optimized for its strengths and design patterns. The serverless approach uses Azure Functions in an event-driven model, and on the container side, containerized microservices are employed with appropriate orchestration patterns.
Performance is measured using k6, a free load testing tool that enables consistent and repeatable test scenarios for both implementations. Test scenarios are constructed to evaluate specific metrics: response time under load, resource utilization, scaling behavior, and cold start characteristics where applicable.
The test plan incorporates progressive load patterns to simulate realistic traffic conditions from light background usage to burst traffic scenarios. Each test plan has multiple runs to ensure statistical significance of results and account for variation in cloud service performance.
The cost analyses combine actual resource consumption data with current Azure pricing models to provide realistic total cost of ownership calculations. Various usage scenarios are modeled to better understand how cost characteristics vary across different traffic patterns and application requirements.
Qualitative evaluation relies on a comprehensive account of the implementation process, including the challenges faced, their resolution, and lessons captured during development and deployment. These experiences have been systematically captured to provide an understanding of the context for working with each platform.
Data collection follows existing performance testing conventions, being careful to note variables that could impact or influence results, such as testing location, time of day, and concurrent activities or actions. Results are validated through multiple testing runs and statistical analysis.

# Review of Literature and Conceptual Framework

## 2.1 Automating the Software Development Life Cycle

The topic of Software Development Life Cycle (SDLC) automation has transformed significantly since its early history in software engineering. The earlier SDLC models with sequential phases and manual transitions to testing and operations have given way to more integrated models focused on continuous delivery and rapid iteration.
DevOps has emerged as both a cultural movement and a set of technical practices that have transformed how software development teams approach the development life cycle. Conway's research on organizational structure and system design helped establish the foundation for understanding how organizational structures can influence the architecture of software solutions, which aligns well with modern cloud-native development practices. Humble and Farley in their pioneering book on continuous delivery described the DevOps movement and identified the deployment pipeline as a central mechanism for automating the journey from source code to production environments.
Modern SDLC automation consists of several key practices that define today's technology sector. Continuous Integration, which was first described and used by Beck and Fowler in their work on Extreme Programming, focuses on frequently integrating code changes into a shared repository along with automated tests to quickly identify integration errors early in the development life cycle. Continuous Integration later evolved into more advanced automated builds, static code analysis, and security scanning as part of the integration process. Continuous Deployment takes the concept of automation further by automatically promoting changes to progressively higher environments until they reach production when automated checks pass. This method requires solid testing practices, sufficient observability, and capable rollback options to deliver features quickly while maintaining system stability.
Infrastructure as Code has been one of the most significant enablers of the automation movement in the software development life cycle (SDLC) era. It allows infrastructure provisioning and configuration to be managed with code under version control rather than being manually created. Kief Morris's work on infrastructure automation has shown that thinking about infrastructure as software allows us to utilize the same quality practices of software development to manage infrastructure. This has been especially relevant in cloud computing, where software controls the infrastructure, enabling full automation of resource provisioning and configuration.
The practice of immutable infrastructure (where infrastructure components are replaceable, not modifiable) has become increasingly popular among organizations in an effort to limit configuration drift and improve system reliability. Immutable infrastructure patterns correlate with and complement containerization technology and cloud-native deployment patterns.
Test automation has expanded from unit and integration testing for application code to performance testing, security testing, and chaos engineering practices. Moving to microservices architectures has added complexity to testing distributed systems, while also providing new methodologies that include contract testing, service virtualization, and enhanced observability (including detecting problems at service boundaries).
As automated systems have become commonplace, observability has become another critical aspect of systems operation. Observability is distinguished from monitoring by including agents and components within the system/application as part of the observation process. Observability extends beyond monitoring when additional practices such as distributed tracing, structured logs, and metrics are operationalized to observe behavior across complex architectures and distributed systems. The three observability pillars - metrics, logs, and traces - offer comprehensive visibility into system behavior to enable automated detection and response to operational problems.
## 2.2 Cloud Computing Paradigms

Cloud computing has evolved through several layers of abstraction and levels of management responsibility, with each stage producing a unique paradigm. This discussion provides context for how serverless and containers, as two cloud paradigms of application deployment and management, influence an organization's DevOps practices.
Infrastructure as a Service represents the lowest-level layer of cloud computing, providing access to virtualized compute, storage, and networking resources on demand. IaaS offerings, such as Amazon EC2, Google Compute Engine, and Azure Virtual Machines, provide provider-managed underlying physical hardware, but still give substantial control over the operating system, runtime environment, and application stack. This cloud usage model is suited for organizations that desire fine-grained control over the computing environment, or organizations that are migrating existing applications with minimal modification.
Platform as a Service represents a more abstract layer than IaaS, giving users the ability to utilize managed runtime environments to deploy applications without needing to manage infrastructure components. Early PaaS offerings, such as Heroku and Google App Engine, were examples of simpler deployment models utilized in early cloud adoption, though they generally involved significant tradeoffs when architecting applications since those early PaaS implementations had limited flexibility in terms of application architecture and runtime environment.
Over time, PaaS has evolved to provide more flexible offerings that can support different programming languages, frameworks, and architectural patterns, while still providing the primary benefit from a developer's perspective: reducing the amount of platform management required. Modern PaaS solutions typically use container technologies internally while offering a more accessible deployment interface to developers. In contrast, Software as a Service (SaaS) represents the highest level of abstraction and offers complete applications over the internet without developers needing to install or manage applications. Although SaaS is not directly relevant to custom application development, it is important to recognize its role in the broader trend toward managed services and operational simplicity. Function as a Service has emerged as a new category that sits between PaaS and SaaS, offering event-driven compute resources with built-in auto-scaling and fine-grained billing models. 
FaaS represents a more extreme form of managed services by having the cloud provider manage everything from infrastructure and runtime management to scaling, leaving the developer to focus solely on implementing business logic. 
Container as a Service has also emerged as another strategy that preserves developer control over application runtime environments while taking advantage of managed orchestration environments. Container as a Service offerings such as Azure Kubernetes Service, Google Kubernetes Engine, and Amazon Elastic Kubernetes Service offer developers the flexibility of containerized applications combined with managed operational infrastructure. Each of these paradigms reflects a shared responsibility model that varies considerably, with each providing a different level of operational concern transferred from the customer to provider. In IaaS deployments, the customer is responsible for security configuration, operating system patching, and managing the application runtime environment. 
PaaS alleviates some of these operational concerns by managing the runtime environment, and FaaS relieves nearly all operational concerns except for the application code. Multi-cloud and hybrid strategies are growing in popularity for organizations that want to avoid vendor lock-in and take advantage of best-of-breed services across multiple providers. Multi-cloud and hybrid cloud approaches introduce additional complexities regarding networking normalization, identity management, and data synchronization across different service providers, but can mitigate risk and provide strategic flexibility to an organization. 
Edge computing is also an emerging paradigm that applies cloud computing principles to locations closer to end users or data sources. Edge deployments generally still utilize container technologies due to their portability and efficiency characteristics, making edge computing relevant for understanding the larger computing ecosystem that encompasses serverless and container technologies.
## 2.3 Serverless Architecture

Serverless computing signifies a fundamental shift from the server-based mindset that pervades traditional designs, even though its title somewhat misleadingly implies servers do not exist. The serverless paradigm shifts the focus from managing infrastructure to implementing pure business logic, as the cloud provider undertakes all server provisioning, scaling, and maintenance considerations.
The theoretical underpinnings of serverless computing come from various computer science concepts, including event-driven programming, microservices architectures, and reactive systems architectures. Event-driven architectures have been studied extensively by distributed systems researchers, and these architectures serve as inspiration for serverless function execution, where a function responds to a particular trigger or event rather than running continuously.
Function as a Service (FaaS) is the primary implementation model of serverless computing, with distinguishing characteristics unlike traditional deployment models. Functions typically execute in stateless environments with bounded execution duration, requiring developers to employ specific programming patterns that account for these characteristics. Since function execution environments are short-lived, external state must be managed using databases, message queues, or other persistent services.
One of the most interesting characteristics of serverless platforms is cold start behavior, which occurs when a function needs to be initialized in a new execution environment. The cold start process involves many behind-the-scenes operations, including starting up the container, initializing the execution runtime, and loading application code into the execution environment, all of which contribute several hundred milliseconds of added latency when serving a request. Cold start behavior has been extensively researched to develop various strategies that reduce function startup time, including connection pooling, optimizing the cold-start initialization process, predicting cold starts based on traffic patterns, and warming functions based on serverless traffic patterns.
The continuous auto-scaling nature of serverless platforms allows applications to handle significant or dramatic traffic variations without pre-provisioning resources. Serverless architectures and platforms are fundamentally distinct from auto-scaling approaches built around known performance and scaling criteria. For example, a serverless platform can scale functions to zero when not invoked and then create thousands of concurrent invocations if demand exists. This different scaling approach can represent significant cost savings in computing expenses and resource consumption efficiency.
Common serverless pricing structures are usage-based, where billing is related to function execution duration and resource consumption. This pricing structure can provide savings with sporadic function use but may not save as much money for applications with constant, predictable loads.
The serverless ecosystem has grown to include substantial additional services that wrap around core FaaS services. Various event sources such as HTTP gateways, message queues, database triggers, or file events can trigger function invocations. Other services provide infrastructure connections to database services, API services, or cloud services, eliminating the need for manual connection building or coding.


While serverless computing varies vendor to vendor, because of the differences in implementations, they have similarities in thinking about what capabilities, performance aspects, and pricing structures are to be constructed. AWS Lambda was the first widely adopted FaaS service and thus was the benchmark for the following services, but multiple vendors have similar, differing features. Azure Functions interfaces with its Azure ecosystem and Google Cloud Functions has a significant development speed, simplicity, and reduced complexity focus.


These variations in serverless development also create the need to design use cases, with different patterns of design, to account for the stateless and ephemeral nature of the function executions. Patterns of a clean separation of concerns, formal error handling, to use out of band services for state and persistence, tracking cold start behavior, performance optimizations and other similar consideration use cases, are examples of this. Security in a serverless architecture may involve the shared responsibility model, the proper management of secrets and credentials, and appropriate access controls for function triggers and integrations.


The serverless architecture can make observability challenging due to function execution being ephemeral and the distributed architecture of serverless applications. Traditional monitoring solutions may not provide adequate visibility into the performance of functions, and dedicated tools and approaches may be necessary to logging, collecting metrics data, and provide distributed tracing across function boundaries.
## 2.4 Container Orchestration and Kubernetes
Container orchestration has its roots in research on virtualization on trusted operating systems. Container-based applications have evolved chiefly around process isolation and resource management on a single host, building off of existing research. The introduction of Docker accelerated the use of containers in production by providing useable tools and a standard format to define containers. However, there was research and practice in virtualization on trusted operating systems prior to containers being a process in the world.


As development moved beyond a single host deployment of containers into development and orchestration of containers across multi-host infrastructure, there became a need for a broad platform to deploy and manage containerized applications on distributed infrastructure. It could be argued that Docker Swarm and Apache Mesos were the earliest orchestration tools, but have different approaches to container scheduling, networking, and management in addition to the work being done by proprietary solution providers.


Kubernetes emerged from the experience of Google in internal container-orchestration, developing through experience and years of managing workloads in containers as information technology solutions at scale, and even had their own internal container orchestration system called Borg and Omega. Kubernetes’s open-source platform empowered the greater community with orchestration capabilities that were previously only available to well-resourced technology companies.


Kubernetes architecture is based on a master-worker model. The control plane runs as a master and maintains the state of the cluster, while the worker nodes simply run the containerized workloads. In a major change from traditional infrastructure management, Kubernetes adopted a declarative model for workload management, whereby users define the desired state instead of using imperative commands. Kubernetes's declarative approach really enables automation like self-healing, rolling updates, and automated scaling based on metrics.
Kubernetes's pod abstraction is the most basic deployment unit. A pod usually contains one or more tightly-bound containers with shared networking and storage. The pod abstraction allows for co-located processes, while still having separation between different application parts. Each pod's lifecycle is managed, from scheduling to starting to health-checking and terminating, with rules to apply if a pod is ready, unready, or needs to be restarted.


Services are an abstraction that provides stable networking identities for pods, along with a level of physical decoupling in that it’s more difficult to access by client. Kubernetes defines several different types: ClusterIP (default and only available from within the cluster), NodePort (available on each worker node's IP), LoadBalancer (for external clients to access the pods as an external service through a load balancer), and ExternalName (a service that points to an external service such as an external database). There are others that are more advanced, but these cover basic service deployment and networking concepts.


The challenge Kubernetes addresses is persistent data storage for containerized applications. The Persistent Volume abstraction allows storage provisioning to be separate from pod lifecycle, so the data within a pod can persist even if the pod is stopped or moved to another worker node. Persistent Volumes decouple storage provisioning from the consuming pod or pods. Storage Classes allows forward dynamic provisioning capabilities that abstract the storage resource types, whether it is traditional or cloud-based storage.


Configuration in native Kubernetes is accomplished through ConfigMaps and Secrets, which allows application configuration to be externalized from container images while still following good guidance using twelve-factor app methodology. Storing the application config outside allows the same container images to run similar applications across environments with different configuration.


The Kubernetes ecosystem is vast, and it includes a great number of extension mechanisms, whether through Custom Resource Definitions, Operators, or admission controllers, as a means to customize Kubernetes or integrate with other systems. This flexibility and extensibility also provide the community the ability to create a very large number of third-party components and services to improve the basic Kubernetes utility for use case scenarios.


Kubernetes security involves layers, including ensuring security from image container vulnerabilities, network policies, role-based access control, and pod security policies, is complex, and there is a growing trend for tools and practices to cover container/image vulnerability scanning, policy enforcement, checking compliance.


For cloud providers with managed Kubernetes services, they are addressing the operational complexity of running a Kubernetes cluster and the ease of using Kubernetes. Many services are offered, including AWS EC2, GKE, or AKS, focused on the customer management and provisioning of the control plane, managing the (virtual) machine nodes, and also managing the periodic upgrading of the Kubernetes version they are running.


Finally, Kubernetes operational complexity has drove companies to develop sophisticated monitoring, logging, or troubleshooting tools. Some baseline level of monitoring, metrics, and logging have been developed in projects like: Prometheus for metrics collection and monitoring, Grafana for visualization, and numerous logging aggregation tools like Loki.

## 3.1 Research Design and Approach
This research employs a comparative case study method that uses functionally the same applications deployed under both serverless and container-based architectures. Instead of simply theoretical evaluation or synthetic benchmarks, this research uses practical configuration – source coding and measuring performance empirically from tangible development experience.
The experimental design was based upon an empirically replicated experiment comparing implementation on both platforms with the same functional requirements, so that implementation, operational characteristics, and performance behaviors could undergo direct comparisons. This kind of controlled experiment accounts for factors that had to do with functionality – thus making meaningful attribution for differences seen to underlying platform characteristics much clearer.
Comparative implementation methodology was selected based upon the understanding that the available literature inevitably provides piecemeal bits of information from different sources and at different levels based on metrics or speculation of theoretical evaluation. As their name suggests, real-world architectural decisions needed developing context to hold whether performance characteristics are established only during run time or as a systems performance characteristics. This research drew on complete applications with realistic forensic complexity to assess the rich spectrum of compromises builders and organizations weigh when selecting between either.
The experimental design considers quantitative information and qualitative observations acquired in the course of development implementation. Performance offered quantitative descriptive evaluative facts for comparison during implementation, while documentation of development experience offered affordance contrasts that are hard to present in a quantitative nature but important cone of expertise discoveries for practical decision-making gains.
Implementations for each platform were entirely developed independently, utilizing best practices for that platform and not by specifying an identical platform architecture. This approach ensured that each implementation exploited the benefits of the specific platform rather than simulating constraints caused to produce conflicted benefits towards either a platform. The research methodology recognizes that we cannot consider technology evaluation separately from its context. So, the research explores not only runtime characteristics, but also development workflow effectiveness, operational complexity, and how it aligns with modern software development lifecycle approaches.
The data collection methodologies were developed to identify both steady-state behaviors and dynamic characteristics such as scaling traits and failure recovery. Multiple runs of the tests were conducted to account for variability in the cloud service executions and provide a level of statistical confidence with the measurements.
The methodology draws on past lessons learned from comparative studies and addresses noted weaknesses such as over-dependence on synthetic workloads and lack of operational emphasis. This research provides an improved basis for architectural decision-making with the implementation of real applications which included substantial elements of authentication, data persistence and user interface.
## 3.2 Application Architecture Design
The application architecture was designed to represent typical modern web applications, while staying focused enough to allow detailed study during the course of research. It includes standard components found in most business applications: user authentication, data persistence, API services, and user interfaces capable of responsive design.
The underlying application concept is a blog management system which has fantastic support for both publicly available and authenticating user interactions. This domain was selected because it included standard patterns such as read-heavy workloads, authenticated operations, and content management workflows that are also found in most business applications. The system architecture utilizes a three tier structure that separates presentation, application logic, and data persistence layers. This structure allows for independent scaling and optimization of each tier, applying clear demarcations that facilitate maintenance and evolution. The frontend layer is based on React and typescript in order to form a modern single page application with client-side routing and responsive layout. The use of React indicates the direction of the industry, and typescript improves type safety that is in service of developer priorities and maintainability. The frontend communicates to backend services through a RESTful API using typical HTTP verbs and JSON to transfer metadata.
The backend services expose a RESTful API that features authentication, authorization, and data access interfaces. The REST API design with RESTful services provides clear resource identification and use appropriateness of HTTP status codes. Business features are described and handled in service layers abstracted from the database. The service layers provide domain protection, orthogonal error handling, and logging.
Data persistence leverages PostgreSQL managed as a cloud data service because of its features, ACID conformance and appropriate performance. There are multiple database tables and schemas that hold blog posts, user info, categories and metadata. The schema is managed through database migrations to allow for future enhancements and automated deployment.
The authentication architecture is different between the two implementations to take advantage of strengths of the platforms. In the serverless implementation, Azure AD B2C provides a fully managed identity service; while for the Kubernetes implementation, Keycloak serves as self-hosted identity management that uses OAuth2 and OpenID Connect.
The system has a planned log and monitoring capability so that there are insights to the performance characteristics and behaviors of the application. The structured logging provides a means of analyzing events that occur in the application in an efficient manner via defining a standard format for an application event; while collecting metrics provides quantitative insight to the performance of the application.
Secured elements are considered throughout all of the architecture including how authentication tokens are managed, secured communications, input validation, and what access control levels are applied. Each implementation adheres to secure best practices appropriate to the platform.
Additionally, there are health check endpoints in the architecture to provide information on whether the application is up and running. There are simple availability checks and detailed system status information, like database connectivity, that can inform operational decisions.
Error handling is designed to facilitate graceful degradation under failures, while logging and monitoring should have enhanced visibility to the failure cases. Fault handling in the architecture involves retry logic, and if interacted with by end users, a circuit breaker pattern used where appropriate.

The technology choices place emphasis on tools/frameworks that are a common part of the enterprise landscape to reinforce the research is usable for real-life decision making situations experienced by development teams and business organizations
## 3.4 Evaluation Framework
The evaluation framework focuses on multiple dimensions of comparison provide an encompassing view of the relative merits and weaknesses of each approach. The framework does not consider only the runtime performance but the whole development and operations experience.
Implementation complexity evaluation looks at the effort required to setup the development environment, configuration of deployment pipelines and implement equivalent functionality. This encompasses both objective measures, such as lines or configuration code and subjective measures - developer experience, and learning curve needs.
Performance evaluation uses structured load testing with scripted scenarios that simulate realistic usage patterns. The load testing scenarios traverse from light background loads through moderate sustained loads to bursts of load that examine scaling response. Each load test implies measuring response times, throughput characteristics and usage patterns.
The load testing methodology uses k6 with progressively growing load patterns that examine both steady-state performance and dynamic scaling behavior. The number of virtual users progress from a single user to moderate loads to bursts of user load testing of 15 concurrent users which reveals the performance characteristics at progressive load levels.
Specific load test types are designed to strain specific aspects of the system - CPU intensive behavior, memory allocation behavior, and types of database interactions. Each workload type supports configurable complexity levels to help analyse how platform attributes and different computional demands interact.
Cost analysis examines both direct resource costs and ongoing operational overhead costs across different platforms. Direct costs are based on the actual resources used in testing and the pricing models associated with the cloud services. Operational costs also weigh the labour utilized monitoring, maintinance and trouble-shooting.
The cost analysis approach simulated scenarios with different utilizations including bursty traffic patterns, steady-state baseload handling, and peak usage conditions. This helped to examine and observe how costs are impacted by usage scenarios and understand where pricing models would break-even with each other.

## 4.6 Challenges and Solutions
The serverless implementation had some challenges that can be characterized into multiple categories, with the variety of chosen architecture and technical solution respectively; providing empirical experience with serverless development that exceeds theoretical experience.


Cold start was the largest performance concern, being especially the most apparent for the first instance after idle. Function initialization was within 300-800ms, depending on how complex the dependencies were and how it connected to the database. Instead of aiming to completely eliminate cold starts, we focused on managing dependencies, pooling connections, and managing user expectations on performance.


Pulling database connections in ephemeral execution environments proved more difficult than expected. Traditional pooling strategies used for long-living applications had to be re-thought for connection lifecycle that varies this highly in function-based architectures. In our scenario, we opted for singleton pools with valid timeout and connection validation strategies that provided for efficiency but still provided reliability.


The complexity of the local development environment was beyond what was expected including the need for installing the Azure Functions Core Tools, installing the local database, and setting up authentication services. The development workflow took a lot more effort than traditional web applications did and hence necessitated copious amounts of documentation and automated setups in order to create an environment setup that was as close to standard as possible.


The debugging of serverless functions had to utilize a different approach from traditional applications with debugging. Local debugging capabilities were limited, however production troubleshooting was done from structured logging and telemetry data instead of interactive debugging. This was managed by using more strategic logging techniques and comprehensive Application Insights integration.


Configuration management for multi-function scenarios involved coordination to allow for consistent behavior but allow for specific function customization. The approach we settled on was using shared configuration modules, applying environment variable mgmt. strategies, and applying configuration validation techniques to minimize runtime configuration dependency issues.


Testing strategies did require adjustments to fit the Azure Functions runtime model especially for integration testing that had to simulate the execution environment of an Azure Function. The solution involved bespoke testing utilities, and the mocking of Azure-specific features.


Error management in distributed function architectures required strategies more advanced than monolithic applications. Correlating errors and debugging functions was complicated, requiring structured logs, correlation strategies, and error management patterns providing adequate diagnostic detail.


Dependency management required consideration of size and initialization time impacts on cold start performance. The larger the size of the dependency trees, the longer it takes to start the function, and has to be addressed through dependency analysis, the right packages, and build optimization processes to minimize runtime overhead.


Performance optimization required an understanding of memory allocation in Azure Functions, runtime environment behavior and scaling patterns. Performance optimization patterns for traditional applications had to be modified for serverless execution characteristics, resulting in optimization patterns specific to functions.


Cost monitoring and optimization requires understanding the consumption-based billing model and its usage patterns. Cost forecasting proved to be complicated due to the variability of serverless billing and was dealt with implementation of monitoring and usage analysis tooling.


State management across function invocations required external persistence mechanisms, as functions cannot persist state between invocations. This architectural constraint was resolved with sound database design and caching strategies; in-memory state management is generally not feasible.


Securing stateless environments required token-based authentication strategies and proper secret management. The solution provided included OAuth 2.0 token validation, secret environment variables are kept secure, and the use of managed identity services.


The coordination of deployments between functions and supporting services requires orchestration to ensure the application state is stable during the update process. This is managed with deployment sequencing, health checks during deployments, and rollback processes to ensure service availability is managed during the deployment process.


Monitoring and observability in distributed serverless architectures required more significant instrumentation than was had with traditional applications. The solution provided included structured log implementation, custom metric collection, and monitoring dashboards for operational visibility.


The learning curve for serverless development was steeper than initially anticipated, especially for developers with experience with traditional web application development. My project team managed this by developing documentation, sample implementations, and training to build serverless knowledge while being productive quickly.

## 6.3 Cost Analysis
The cost assessment between serverless and Kubernetes methods yielded complicated tradeoffs that were highly dependent on application usage patterns, application properties, and operational requirements. The costs were analyzed against service costs from Azure only, leaving out TCO, which involves other factors such as development time, operational overheads, and expertise requirements for the team.
Pricing Model Basics
Azure Functions uses a consumption-based pricing model, where the costs are only fired off based on how long the function was running, plus how much resource you consumed when it was. The pricing includes a charge based on execution time (GB seconds consumed), how many times it ran total, and data transfer, enabling variable costs based on actual process usage.
For the Kubernetes approach, there are still charges that are incurred based on it being an AKS cluster, virtual machines, and underlying infrastructure, which don't change regardless of how much the application is used. This allows for predictable monthly costs but could also incur cost while allowing for idle resources during low traffic.
Cohort Resource Cost Breakdown
According to the testing scenarios we undertook, the direct resource costs during the 14-minute test period, inclusive of ~1428 requests, were around $0.000286 for Azure Functions and ~$0.000234 for Kubernetes, suggesting it was marginally cheaper. However, we must be careful to interpret that comparison since, as mentioned earlier, the way costs were laid out is so fundamentally different between the costs.
Azure Functions cost estimates included usage base solely too the time of function execution and any associated costs, and for Kubernetes, they took costs on an ongoing basis, running constantly. The Kubernetes-based service would be preferred for big traffic load applications on a Kubernetes-based service. For services that see intermittent or sporadic traffic characteristics, the serverless based option would have the edge on cost since you don’t have to pay for anything that is not being utilized along the spikes of usage.
Break-even Analysis
The break-even calculation compares actual observed monthly costs to determine when each platform becomes more cost-effective:

**Observed Monthly Costs:**
- **Azure Functions**: $18/month (base + variable costs)
- **AKS Kubernetes**: $95/month (fixed infrastructure)
- **Cost difference**: $77/month

**How Azure Functions Pricing Works:**
Azure Functions charges based on:
1. **Execution time**: $0.000016 per GB-second (memory × duration)  
2. **Execution count**: $0.20 per million function calls

**Cost Per Request:**
Using test data (127ms execution, 0.5GB memory):
```
Per request = (0.127s × 0.5GB × $0.000016) + ($0.20 ÷ 1M)
            = $0.000001016 + $0.0000002 = $0.000001216
```

**Break-even Calculation:**
```
When do Azure Functions monthly costs = Kubernetes monthly costs?
Azure Functions cost = $0.000001216 × number of requests
Kubernetes cost = $95/month (fixed)

Setting them equal:
$0.000001216 × requests = $95
requests = $95 ÷ $0.000001216 = 78.1 million requests/month
```

**What This Means:**
- **Under 78.1M requests/month**: Azure Functions cheaper (variable costs < $95 fixed)
- **Over 78.1M requests/month**: Kubernetes cheaper (fixed $95 < growing Functions costs)

This explains why high-volume applications favor Kubernetes' predictable costs, while variable traffic benefits from Functions' pay-per-use model. The analysis excludes database, networking, and operational costs.
Operational Cost Implications
Aside from the direct Azure service charges, the operational cost is a major factor of the total cost of ownership. The serverless implementation minimizes operational costs because of the managed service capability, but may have a higher per unit cost because of the cost associated with the fully managed services.
Implementing Kubernetes incurs a greater operational black space including monitoring, maintenance, and troubleshooting overhead, but gives teams more discretion in the areas of resource efficiency and cost management. Teams that onboard Kubernetes and already operating Kubernetes may offset operational costs through better resource planning and resource efficiency.

Both implementations share the same Azure PostgreSQL Flexible Server instance, so therefore had the same database costs, which do not influence the cost analysis comparisons. The connection patterns to the database differ from implementations, which may have bearing on connection limitations and performance improvements, and may have implications on database sizing and cost. The connection pooling issues of a serverless implementation could lead to higher database connections of the data service, and ultimately affect the selection of the database tier and pricing. Kubernetes deployments allow for a more traditional connection pooling solution and could help optimize the resources of the database.
Storage and Data Transfer Pricing
Storage costs were low for both methods, with container registry costs and function storage costs being a very small piece of the overall infrastructure value. Data transfer costs were also kept low for the testing scenarios, however production applications that do significant data transfer may not have the same pricing structure.

Azure Functions have an auto-scaling feature that is built into the function application and the scalability is handled for the developer by the platform – the instances, load balancing, and all backend resource requests are handled automatically based on the number of requests the platform receives. Consequently, automatic scaling configurations are never required, and scaling behavior is rate sensitive to the incoming request patterns across the platform.
The Kubernetes deployment implementation used only fixed single replica deployments – even with horizontal pod auto-scaling – with deliberate simplicity to improve clarity of implementation over scalability. This type of deployment may at least provide examples of moving mannequin scaling behavior; however, the Kubernetes implementation noted that simple deployment configurations can sometimes maintain workloads for completely acceptable scenarios.

Observing load testing of the Azure Functions auto-scaling behavior gave new function instances of use as requests increased. The auto-scaling seemed to respond within seconds of load increases, but further investigation was a non-factor about conducting load testing at moderate levels.

Resource Utilization Behavior
Scalability at the function level, would allow for capacity management in a fine-grain fashion per each individual function. This granular scaling allows for the use of common resource allocations without explicitly managing resources or worrying about capacity planning.
Scaling in Kubernetes would take place at the pod level where the scaling of the entire application, including all of the functionly, would be treated as a single unit. This is less granular than scaling at the function level while benefiting from the scaling of shared and stateful components which may not be scalable as isolated units.

Azure Functions scaling has limits and quotas imposed by the platform for maximum scale. Additionally, the cold start experience can suffer if multiple function instance are awakened from cold start simultaneously.
Simplifying Kubernetes configuration eliminated aspects of scaling delays yet also removed degrees of flexibility to dynamically address resource needs when scaling changed. A realistic rollout of a production deployment of Kubernetes would allow for pods achieve horizontal autoscaling based upon metrics from CPU usage, memory used, or custom metrics to accomplish scaling behaviour.

Serverless functions often introduces more variable connection patterns due to instance creation and destruction, whilst containerized applications facilitate more predictable connection pooling patterns.
Cost Implications of Scaling
Serverless scaling provides additional cost benefits by adjusting utilization of resources based on actual utilization. The model deducts cost for better resource utilization, in the case there is excess capacity being utilized. Serverless scaling makes sense particularly when you have applications that are susceptible to massive traffic spikes and/or somewhat unpredictable usage patterns.
Kubernetes scaling requires sophisticated cost management strategies, and the effective sizing of node pools, resource limit definitions, and scaling policies, where there is tradeoffs of performance requirements versus cost expectations. Although this is more operationally expensive, it allows you better granularity in managing costs versus resource utilization.

The serverless model eliminates operational complexities of scaling configuration, monitoring, and management. Application developers can focus entirely on their business logic implementation, and not on the scalability decisions or resource management.
Kubernetes scaling (for the most part) offers operational complexities in configuring your horizontal pod autoscaler, collecting the correct metrics, and tuning your scaling policy. This requires specialized expertise and ongoing management, but offers more control in scaling behavior and resource utilization optimization.

Built-in observability provides a good way to evaluate using representative data and prepare for scaling optimization.

## 8.1 Research Questions Answered

This research study initiated with a broad question: “How do serverless architectures with Azure Functions compare to container-based with Azure Kubernetes Service, in terms of implementation complexity, performance characteristics, operational requirements, and cost efficiency for modern web applications?” After months of building, running, and analyzing products with the data, I’m pleased to present empirical findings supported by true implementation experience, not theory.
Implementation Complexity Results
Implementation complexity analysis revealed nuanced trade-offs rather than simple "easier to harder" comparisons. The Azure Functions implementation required 40% less infrastructure configuration and enabled productive development within hours rather than days. However, trade-offs included limited debugging capabilities, minimal local development experience, and constrained performance tuning capabilities that could be frustrating when optimization was needed.
Conversely, Kubernetes had higher upfront complexity, but provided complete transparency and control which proved important for debugging and performance issues. The upfront time investment to learn containerization and orchestration lifecycle terminology proved worthwhile and provided significant savings over the development lifecycle. A complexity cost analysis should consider not just short-term project costs, but longer-term capability potential.
Performance Characteristics
The performance characteristic that I found the most surprising was for Azure Functions, it always performed better, based off response timing - 359ms average compared to K8s 712ms and it got worse - like at 95% of responses. This was opposite my initial assumptions from theoretical benefits from containerized implementations.
As stated, cold-start behavior became a serious operational performance issue in this case, with initialization times of roughly 300ms - 800ms which would completely negate the response time of the irregular traffic app. The performance comparison conversation was far more nuanced than basic benchmark comparisons, where real software attributes dictated which method created the best user experience.
Raw performance was nearly equal for both platforms (14.5 million ops/second vs 14.7 million ops/second); meaning the performance variation was primarily due to platform overhead and optimization strategies rather than out-of-box compute capacity.
Operational Requirements Evaluation
In terms of evaluation of operational SIM complexities, platform management requirements fully aligned with organizational capabilities and appetite. With serverless the traditional infra management was spared at the cost of operational visibility and troubleshooting ability. Teams gained auto scale benefits, and streamlined deployment practices, but were lost when it became time for production debugging of incidents or custom optimizations.
Ordering K8s operational requirements were highly daunting, but provided unequivocal visibility and control, which was important for complex applications and operational contexts. The time investment in learning how to manage K8s effectively matured organizational capabilities that could be reused across projects and technology stacks.
Cost Benefit Analysis
Cost savings analysis indicated that simple break-even estimates (in this case, ~ 365 million requests) usually mute economic considerations that influence the decision of platform selection. Under the serverless pay-as-you-go scheme, was a dramatic advantage given apps with variable or unpredictable traffic flow, while the K8s fixed costs were favored for organizations seeking greater economic and resource efficiency.
What may be worth just as much is realizing that operational costs, development velocity impacts, and opportunity costs may out-weigh the direct infrastructure costs for total cost of ownership realized. The choice of economic solution would largely depend on organizational circumstance, team capacity, and application feature rather than any universal cost metric.
Secondary Research questions
The secondary research questions ended up leading to wider view perspectives that were insightful for understanding contextual platform selection justifications. CI/CD Pipeline complexity has major deviations amongst the contexts, serverless enjoyed ease of deployment workflows, whereas K8s had more complex orchestration utilizing a linked container image and manifest coupling.
Security and authentication evaluation indicated clear trade-offs between managed service utility (Azure AD B2C) vs operational ownership (self-hosted Keycloak). Each had strong security services, but with operational trade-off the customization potential aligned to organization circumstances and risk appetites.
For developer experience considerations, my study validated that productivity outputs for an enterprise of teams is often determined by team experience and application characteristics. Teams in a serverless context saw a boost in velocity initially while K8s teams out of a traditional container context saw benefit from K8s flexibility ultimately thanks to a more rigorous epic based development methodology. The organizational learning variability on the other hand was more significant than I suspected before as well, particularly for developers who were experiencing containerization for the first time.
